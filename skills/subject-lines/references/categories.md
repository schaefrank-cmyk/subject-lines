# The nine categories

Each entry: what it is, when it earns its place, worked examples with the
mechanism named, and the failure mode that kills it.

---

## 1. FOMO

Exclusivity and the prospect of losing access you already have. Distinct from
urgency: **FOMO is scarcity of access, urgency is scarcity of time.**

- **"Early access closes Sunday — then it's the public ballot"** — Frames the
  reader as already inside a privileged group and names exactly what they drop
  to if they wait. The loss is concrete rather than implied.
- **"Only 200 spots left in the half"** — A hard number outperforms "limited
  spots." Specificity reads as truth; vagueness reads as marketing.
- **"Last year this sold out in 6 days"** — Social proof doing FOMO's job. Makes
  no claim about this year, so it stays honest even if the race doesn't sell out.
- **"We're holding your 2026 entry until Friday"** — Possession framing. The
  entry is already theirs and inaction takes it away. Loss aversion is stronger
  than gain.

**Failure mode:** manufactured scarcity. If spots aren't actually limited, this
is a lie with a short half-life.

---

## 2. Urgency

A real deadline, price change or closing window. Works because delay has a
measurable cost.

- **"Price goes up at midnight — $89 → $115"** — Shows the actual cost of
  waiting. The arrow does the arithmetic for a reader who is scanning.
- **"Final call: registration closes tonight"** — Plain and front-loaded. The
  category doesn't need cleverness when the deadline is genuinely today.
- **"48 hours left at the early-bird rate"** — A countdown with a reason
  attached. "48 hours left" alone is a threat with no object.
- **"Deferral deadline is Friday — after that your entry's gone"** — Urgency at a
  lifecycle moment rather than a sale. Most programmes only use urgency to sell,
  which is why it stops landing.

**Failure mode:** fake deadlines. The list learns within two or three sends, and
once it decides your deadlines are decorative, the category is dead for that
sender permanently. This is the single most expensive mistake in the playbook.

---

## 3. Curiosity

Opens a loop the reader wants closed. The most durable category, because it
doesn't depend on a deadline or a discount and so doesn't wear out.

- **"The hill at mile 9"** — Specific enough that anyone registered knows what it
  refers to and wants to know what you're about to say. Insider knowledge, no hype.
- **"We changed the course. Here's why."** — Announces a change and withholds the
  reason. Also does real work: course changes generate questions, and this gets
  ahead of them.
- **"Don't start your taper yet"** — A counter-instruction. The reader's plan is
  suddenly in question and the only way to resolve it is to open.
- **"What 4,000 runners got wrong last year"** — Implies a mistake the reader may
  be about to make, with a number that makes it feel documented.

**Failure mode:** the gap doesn't pay off. Buys one click, costs the next twenty.

---

## 4. Pain point

Names a problem the runner actually has and implies you can fix it. Signals that
you understand the sport rather than just selling entries into it.

- **"Still can't nail your fueling?"** — A near-universal frustration, phrased
  the way runners phrase it to each other.
- **"Your long runs shouldn't hurt this much"** — Takes a side. Positions you as
  an advocate rather than a vendor, a different relationship than "20% off."
- **"Nervous about your first 13.1? Read this."** — Targets first-timers
  precisely. The number does the segmenting; experienced runners scroll past,
  which is fine.
- **"Parking at the start line, solved"** — Logistics anxiety is real,
  under-addressed, and disproportionately drives race-week support volume.

**Failure mode:** naming a pain the audience doesn't have, which reads as a
stranger guessing.

---

## 5. Question

A direct question the reader wants to answer, or wants answered.

- **"Ready for race week?"** — Short, front-loaded, slightly provocative. Nobody
  feels entirely ready, which is the point.
- **"What's your goal time?"** — Invites a self-assessment and sets up
  personalised content. Strong ahead of a training-plan send.
- **"Have you picked up your bib yet?"** — A question with an operational answer.
  Reads as a helpful check, not a pitch, and reduces race-morning queues.
- **"Training through the heat — or around it?"** — Poses a choice the reader
  hasn't consciously made, which engages more than one they've already resolved.

**Failure mode:** questions with an obvious yes/no answer the reader can settle
without opening.

---

## 6. Re-engagement

Aimed at lapsed runners — previous finishers, or people who registered once and
didn't return. Cheaper than acquisition and likelier to convert.

- **"You ran with us in 2024. Come back for 2026."** — Reminds them of real
  shared history before asking for anything. The specific year proves you know them.
- **"Your PR is still on our leaderboard"** — Flattering, specific, and creates a
  reason to click that isn't a pitch.
- **"It's been a minute. Here's 20% off your entry."** — When soft approaches
  haven't worked, a plain offer with no pretence. Honest about what it is.
- **"We saved your spot — for now"** — Re-engagement borrowing from FOMO. Use
  once per sequence; the tension collapses on repetition.

**Failure mode:** leading with a discount before re-establishing the
relationship, which trains the segment to wait for discounts.

---

## 7. Personalisation

Uses something the runner *did*, not just their first name. The
highest-performing pattern here — a Spartan Race send that put name, finish time
and rank in the subject line drove a **3x CTR lift**.

- **"Kyra — you finished 412th. Here's your photo set."** — Name plus a real
  result. The result is the hook; the name alone is table stakes and readers
  stopped noticing it years ago.
- **"Your Santa Barbara finish: 1:52:07"** — No adjective, no hook. The data is
  the hook. Nobody who ran that race scrolls past their own time.
- **"You're 12 weeks out, Kyra. Here's the plan."** — Personalised on *timing*
  rather than history. Triggers off the race date, so it scales across a whole
  list without per-runner content.
- **"You've run 3 of our races. This one's new."** — Personalised on relationship
  depth. Reframes the ask as a natural next step rather than a cold pitch.

**Failure mode:** it fails loudly. A broken merge field ("Hi {{first_name}}") or
a wrong finish time does more damage than a generic line ever would. Fallback
value and seed-list test, every time.

---

## 8. Clarity / transactional

The plainest possible statement of what's inside. Under-used because it looks
unexciting, but these routinely out-convert clever lines at logistics moments,
because the reader is actively looking for the information.

- **"You're in. Here's what happens next."** — Confirmation. Resolves the
  anxiety, then promises the next step. The most-read email in the lifecycle;
  don't waste it on a pun.
- **"Your bib number is #4471"** — Zero ambiguity. Gets opened, saved, and
  searched for again on race morning.
- **"Race week: everything you need in one email"** — Promises consolidation,
  which is exactly what a runner wants in the final seven days.
- **"Your finisher photos are ready"** — A plain statement of a thing the reader
  already wants. Urgency or emoji here would only make it look like spam.

**Failure mode:** dressing it up. No emoji, no discount, no exclamation marks.

---

## 9. Social proof / community

Positions the race as something people belong to. Works for acquisition, for
re-engagement, and for the dead period between races when there's nothing to sell.

- **"9,000 runners. One start line."** — Scale as an invitation. Two fragments,
  no verb, entirely front-loaded.
- **"Why 62% of our finishers come back"** — Social proof and a curiosity gap at
  once. Only usable if the number is real.
- **"Meet the runner who's done all 12"** — Human story. Consistently outperforms
  promotional sends in the off-season, when there's no deadline to lean on.

**Failure mode:** invented or unflattering numbers. "Join 400 runners" signals a
small race, whether or not that's the message you wanted.

---

## Choosing between overlapping categories

| Torn between | Choose on |
| --- | --- |
| FOMO vs Urgency | Is the scarce thing *access* (FOMO) or *time* (urgency)? If both, lead with time — it's more concrete. |
| Curiosity vs Question | A question invites the reader to answer. Curiosity makes them want *you* to answer. Pick by who holds the information. |
| Pain point vs Curiosity | Does the reader already know they have this problem? Yes → pain point. No → curiosity. |
| Personalisation vs anything | If real per-runner data exists, use it. It beats every other category. |
